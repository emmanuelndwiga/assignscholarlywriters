APA Referencing Guide (7th Edition): Rules, Examples & Tips

By ScholarlyWriters Editorial Team | Published August 2026 | 10 min read

APA style governs how millions of student papers cite sources and format manuscripts. Published by the American Psychological Association, the current seventh edition (2020) applies across psychology, education, nursing, business, and most social science disciplines worldwide. Yet despite its ubiquity, APA generates more consistent mark deductions than almost any other technical skill — not because it is difficult, but because small rules accumulate: where the period goes, which words get capitalised, when to write “et al.”

This guide covers the complete system in plain language: every in-text citation scenario you will meet, the architecture of a correct reference list, worked examples for common source types, and the errors markers see most often. What Is APA Style and Who Uses It?

APA is an author&ndashdate citation system: sources are identified in the body of your text by author surname and year, and full details appear once, alphabetically, in a reference list at the end. This design keeps prose uncluttered while making sources easy to trace.

You will typically be required to use APA if you study:

  • Psychology — APA's home discipline, applied strictly at all levels
  • Nursing, midwifery, and health sciences — especially in US and Canadian programmes
  • Education and teaching
  • Business and management — many MBA programmes
  • Sociology, criminology, and political science — depending on institution

The seventh edition is now standard everywhere, but some departments still circulate sixth-edition guides — always confirm which version your handbook mandates. Wherever your institution sits on the spectrum, our team provides targeted support across all our academic services.

APA In-Text Citations: The Rules

In-text citations contain two elements only — author and year — plus page numbers for direct quotes. They take one of two forms:

  • Narrative: the author is part of your sentence. Okafor (2021) argued that…
  • Parenthetical: the citation sits in brackets. (Okafor, 2021)

One Author

Cite the surname and year every time the source supports a claim: Adeyemi (2019) found that revision spacing improved recall. Or: Spaced revision improves recall (Adeyemi, 2019).

Two Authors

Always cite both names joined by “and” in narrative citations and an ampersand in parenthetical ones: Kaur and Mensah (2020) or (Kaur & Mensah, 2020). This rule does not change with repeated citations.

Three or More Authors

From the first citation onward, use only the first author's surname followed by “et al.”: (Nguyen et al., 2022). One quirk: if shortening would make two different sources identical — Nguyen, Silva (2021) and Nguyen, Park (2021), both becoming “Nguyen et al.” — cite enough additional surnames to distinguish them, e.g. (Nguyen, Silva, et al., 2021) versus (Nguyen, Park, et al., 2021).

Group Authors (Organisations)

Write the organisation's full name in the first citation, with an abbreviation introduced in brackets if you will abbreviate later: (World Health Organization [WHO], 2023). Subsequent citations use the abbreviation alone: (WHO, 2023). Use the abbreviated form only after introducing it.

No Author or No Date

Move the title into the citation position: (“Climate Resilience Report,” 2024) — note quotation marks around article titles and italics implied for standalone works. When the date is genuinely unavailable, write “n.d.”: (Hargreaves, n.d.). Never fabricate a date from a website's copyright footer without checking it refers to the content itself.

Paraphrasing Versus Quoting

Paraphrases need only author and year. Direct quotes additionally require a locator:

  • Short quotes (fewer than 40 words): run into your text inside double quotation marks, with the page number in the citation. As Chen (2020) concluded, “sleep deprivation degrades emotional regulation before cognitive performance” (p. 114).
  • Block quotes (40 words or more): set the quotation out as an indented block without quotation marks, with the citation after the final punctuation mark.
  • No page numbers: use paragraph numbers, section headings, or timestamps for audiovisual sources: (Bello, 2022, 14:32).

Aim to quote sparingly — markers read heavy quotation as weak paraphrasing skill. Reserve direct quotes for wording so distinctive it cannot be improved by restatement.

Secondary Sources (“As Cited In”)

Ideally you cite only sources you have read. When the original is genuinely unavailable: Vygotsky (1978, as cited in Daniels, 2016), and only Daniels (2016) appears in the reference list. Overusing this construction signals insufficient library effort, so treat it as a last resort.

Citing Multiple Works Together

Order parenthetical groups alphabetically, separated by semicolons: (Adeyemi, 2019; Kaur & Mensah, 2020; Okafor, 2021). Alphabetical order — not chronological — is the rule students most often miss here.

The Reference List: Formatting Rules

The reference list is not a bibliography of everything you consulted — it contains exactly the works cited in your text, no more and no fewer. Master these structural rules:

  • Start on a new page headed “References”, centred and bold.
  • Alphabetise by first author's surname. For works with identical authors, order by year, earliest first.
  • Hanging indent: the first line of each entry sits flush left; subsequent lines indent by 0.5 inches.
  • Double-spacing applies throughout, consistent with APA manuscript formatting generally.
  • Invert all authors' names (surname first) and list up to twenty of them — one of the biggest changes from the sixth edition.
  • DOIs as URLs: present every DOI as a live link beginning https://doi.org/…. If no DOI exists for an online source, give the direct URL.
  • Sentence case for titles: capitalise only the first word, proper nouns, and the first word after a colon in article, book, chapter, webpage, and report titles.

Reference Examples by Source Type

Adapt these patterns rather than copying citation-generator output blindly. Italics are indicated below.

Journal Article with DOI

Okafor, L., Chen, S., & Petrova, M. (2022). Sleep quality and academic performance among first-year undergraduates: A longitudinal study. Journal of Educational Psychology, 114(4), 712–728. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000689

Note the pattern: Authors. (Year). Article title in sentence case. Journal Name in Title Case, Volume(Issue), page range. DOI. The journal name and volume number take italics; the issue number does not.

Whole Book

Bhatt, R. (2021). Qualitative research methods for the social sciences (3rd ed.). Cambridge Academic Press.

Editions appear in parentheses after the title. Publisher location is omitted entirely in the seventh edition.

Book Chapter in an Edited Volume

Mensah, A. (2023). Decolonising the business curriculum. In P. Silva & J. Hartley (Eds.), Rethinking management education (pp. 45–67). Routledge.

Webpage on a Website

National Health Service. (2025, March 12). Adult mental wellbeing: Latest statistics. https://www.nhs.uk/example-page-url

When the author and website share a name — as here — omit the site name element entirely to avoid duplication.

News Website Article

Roberts, T. (2026, January 8). University tuition policies face new scrutiny. The Guardian. https://www.theguardian.com/example-article-url

Organisational Report

World Health Organization. (2024). Global status report on nursing workforce retention. WHO Press. https://www.who.int/example-report-url

Government Document

Department for Education. (2025). Children's social care: National framework for practice standards (Cmd. 9871). UK Government Publications. https://www.gov.uk/example-document-url

Dissertation or Thesis

Ferreira, D. (2024). Remote supervision and postgraduate research wellbeing [Master's dissertation, University of Leeds]. University Repository. https://repository.example.ac.uk/handle/12345

Multiple Works by One Author

List solo-authored works chronologically, then alphabetise co-authored entries after them. Add lowercase letters to same-year works to keep citations unambiguous: (Silva, 2022a), (Silva, 2022b) — and mirror those letters in the reference list.

Common APA Mistakes to Avoid

  • Title case creep. Writing article titles in Title Case because journal databases display them that way. Reference lists demand sentence case.
  • Orphan and ghost entries. Every in-text citation needs a reference entry, and vice versa. Mismatches are the single most common audit failure.
  • Retrieving “Retrieved from” — the seventh edition dropped this phrase. Plain URLs now suffice unless a retrieval date is needed for changing content.
  • Publisher locations — likewise abolished. “(London: Sage)” formatting marks you as following the old edition.
  • Wrong ampersand placement. Use “&amp;” inside parentheses and reference lists; write “and” within narrative sentences.
  • Unformatted DOIs. “doi:10.xxxx” prefixes are outdated; use full https links.
  • Trusting generators. Automatic tools mislabel source types constantly — a webpage becomes a report, a preprint becomes an article. Verify every generated entry against the rules above.
  • Citing the abstract. If you read only an abstract, either retrieve the full text or be transparent about the limitation.

What Changed Between APA 6th and 7th Editions?

If your notes date from earlier studies, update these habits:

  • Up to twenty authors are now listed in references (previously seven, with ellipses for the rest).
  • No publisher location: city and country details disappeared from book entries.
  • No “Retrieved from”: URLs appear bare unless a retrieval date is genuinely required.
  • DOIs formatted as https://doi.org/ links, not “doi:” prefixes or database strings.
  • Three-plus authors collapse to et al. immediately — the old rule listing all authors at first citation is gone.
  • Ebooks cite like print books; the words “eBook,” “Kindle version,” and similar labels no longer appear.
  • Website names included for webpages whose author differs from the site itself.
  • Student papers no longer require running heads (though professional submissions retain them).

Practical Tips That Save Marks

  1. Cite as you write. Building the reference list alongside drafting prevents the end-of-project scramble where sources go missing.
  2. Keep a source log. Record full details, DOI, and access date when you first open a source; reconstructing metadata later wastes hours.
  3. Read your handbook's exceptions. Departments modify APA: some demand retrieval dates on everything, others ban certain source types outright.
  4. Audit before submission. Cross-check every citation against the list mechanically — mark each one off. Ten minutes here routinely saves five percent of a grade.
  5. Learn the patterns, not every case. Once you internalise author-date-sentence-case-italics logic, unfamiliar sources become solvable puzzles rather than lookups.
“Correct APA is invisible; incorrect APA shouts carelessness to every examiner who skims your reference list.”

Mastering these conventions pays forward into dissertations, journal submissions, and professional writing alike.
